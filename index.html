Problem statement

Big grid parts can take over two years to arrive, so utilities must predict years ahead which parts they will need and where to keep them. Guess wrong and customers wait, or millions of dollars sit idle on a shelf.

128 wksAverage lead time for a large power transformer, nearly two and a half years.Wood Mackenzie, Q2 2025, as reported by Foley
$1.295TProjected U.S. utility capital spending, 2026 to 2030. Every project that waits for a part is held up.S&P Global Regulatory Research, as reported by Foley
4 to 6×What utilities say transformers cost compared with before 2022.Renewable Energy World

A storm, in three steps

  1. A storm damages a substation. The utility needs a transformer now.

  2. The nearest warehouse has the wrong size. The right one sits in a warehouse far away that nobody checked.

  3. A new unit takes over two years to order, so the repair waits, or a project slips.

Why this is hard

  1. Failure prediction and stock planning usually live in different teams and different systems.

  2. Hold too many parts and cash is tied up. Hold too few and outages last longer.

  3. The facts that decide it are scattered: asset records, inspections, work orders, inventory, supplier lead times.

The three reasons above are our own analysis, not from the sources below.

Billion-dollar problem, unproven product. The problem is large and documented. Whether this solution wins depends on results with real client data, and similar tools already exist.

      Tested code · synthetic data · held-out years

      We trained a model and tested it on years it never saw

      The code is public. It generates made-up utility data for 900 assets over 8 years, trains on 2019 to 2024, and tests on 2025 and 2026. Everything below comes from running that code. Because we wrote the data generator, these numbers show the pipeline works. They do not show how real equipment behaves.

      Read this before quoting a number.
      • Only 84 failures in the test years, so the percentages are rough.
      • Regions differ in age and storm exposure by design in our generator, so a risk-based policy is bound to help. A real utility may or may not look like this.
      • This is one run of one made-up world. A real pilot would use the client's records.

      What it means for Perficient · our reasoning

      A repeatable offering, not a one-off project

      Four reasons this fits how Perficient already works. These are our own reasoning from public information, and we would like to hear where we are wrong.

      Repeatable

      The same core serves energy and utilities, manufacturing and distribution, industries Perficient already covers.

      Databricks pull-through

      Sensor, inspection and inventory data keeps growing, and it lives and is governed on the platform.

      Recurring work

      Models must be monitored and retrained, so delivery becomes an ongoing service.

      Proof, not a pilot

      Every recommendation is tested against what really happened, which backs the claim that the work runs in production.

      How we would size the value with a client

      Value per year = projects held up by parts × weeks saved per project × cost of one week of delay. The inputs below are placeholders to show the method. Replace them with the client's own numbers.

      Example result: $6,000,000 a year. Placeholder inputs, not a forecast.

      How it grows

      Phase 1

      Prove it small: one asset type in one region. Test the predictions and the stocking advice on data the model has never seen.

      Phase 2

      Widen it to more asset types and regions.

      Phase 3

      Reuse the core in manufacturing and distribution.

      Phase 4

      Learn across clients, only with client permission and strong privacy controls. This is a governance question we would raise early.
